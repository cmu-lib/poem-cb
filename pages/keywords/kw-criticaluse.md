### Framing

AI has been on a head-on collision course with education since its initial public explosion in 2022. Against the backdrop of the increasing corporatization of U.S. education, massive budget reductions, and the dual threats of continued distrust and disinvestment in education [Calderone & Fosnacht, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/00027642221118263), AI usage has become normalized in both higher and P-12 education [Johnson, Seamon, & Seamon, 2024](https://doi.org/10.24059/olj.v28i3.4646). AI implementation in education is collaboratively financially backed by various tech organizations and their partnerships with higher learning institutions. Many colleges and universities have built out various instructional training programs and faculty incentives for implementing AI in the classroom, and ensuring the rapid usage among already enrolled undergraduates and incoming freshmen.

Both P-12 and Higher Ed have experienced major pedagogical disruption, as well as massive structural and financial re-pivoting as institutions rapidly confront new possibilities and challenges wrought by AI [Neumann, Rauschenberger, & Schon, 2023](https://doi.org/10.25968/opus-2978). AI education continues to be largely situated within terms of digital literacy, with an emphasis on acquiring technical proficiency. Higher education is now experiencing a secondary disruption due to AI in the form of task automation and its touted promises of increased productivity, and then most recently, a seemingly student-focused ability to self-create personalized AI chatbots referred to as Agents [Schon et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcomp.2023.1208550). To borrow a sentiment from Jurassic Park, “Your scientist was so preoccupied with whether or not they could, they didn’t stop to think if they should.” Similarly, the training provided to faculty mostly centers on practical implementation methods but lacks a critically reflective lens on AI implementation.

“Critical Use” renders transparent the interoperable, intermediated, intertextual, or humanistic agency involved in shaping AI and enhancing the effect of human decision-making in determining AI output. Critical usage engages in reflection and deep thinking on AI’s societal impact, technical constraints, ethical challenges, and individual agency and responsibility for framing AI interaction. The grounding of AI technology within real-world contexts directly supports the POEM project’s objective of “help[ing] high school and college students develop critical thinking habits as they increase their digital fluency and the ethical awareness needed to advocate for more just and equitable digital futures.”

### Context & Scholarship and Critical Considerations

At a foundational level, “Critical Use” is a bricolage of several concepts within the sphere of higher education. At the forefront of this conceptualization is the core idea of critical thinking. [Faccione (2011)](https://insightassessment.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Critical-Thinking-What-It-Is-and-Why-It-Counts.pdf) firmly defines the knowledge dispositions and cognitive skills of critical thinking, ultimately arriving at the following definition of critical thinking: “We understand critical thinking to be purposeful, self-regulatory judgment which results in interpretation, analysis, evaluation, and inference, as well as explanation of the evidential, conceptual, methodological, criteriological, or contextual considerations upon which that judgment is based” (pg. 27). Traditionally, critical thinking in student education and learning has primarily been centered around the abilities of individuals to analyze and dissect various written, oral, and visual logic. Applying the idea of critical thinking to technology acknowledges a new terrain upon which to think about how external sociocultural attitudes, subject knowledge limitations, and biases are being leveraged to reinforce persistent but constantly evolving dynamics within information navigation.

#### The “Critical” in Critical Use

Critical thinking is crucial as emergent technologies, such as AI, facilitate the increased abstraction in the dynamics between information seekers, knowledge management, and the data being output. As [Prasad (2025)](https://iircj.org/wp-content/uploads/3-Critical-Thinking-in-the-Age-of-Generative-AI.pdf) explains,” students often cannot discern the biases in AI outputs since these tools, just like their input data, show the biases” (pg. 9). As such, one of the defining characteristics of “Critical Use” is the direct contextualization of AI tools and outputs, along with the demystification of AI’s hidden “logic” and knowledge biases, as well as the interconnection between these features and the knowledge generated by the AI. There is a lot of potential overlap with the adjacent keywords “Bias,” “Ethics,” and “Evaluation.” Arguably, all three of these adjacent keywords could be seen as discreet subdomains within the umbrella of “Critical Use.”

However, to articulate some of the distinctions within these keywords, I posit that critical thinking and, therefore, “Critical Use” is more about asserting both human agency and responsibility (see [Knight, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/10350330.2020.1836815)) within technological implementation and manipulation. Whereas “Evaluation” delves into more specific frameworks for critically engaging AI output and understanding the relationship between what the user requested and what the machine interpreted from the user’s request. Criticality comes into evaluation when we ascertain the degree of closeness to the desired outcomes sought by the information seeker. 

#### Bias and Ethics in Criticality

“Bias” may get into more of a focus on the types of biases in knowledge management built into AI processing and retrieval. Criticality here lies in the analysis domain, specifically drawing on a more holistic view of AI output and then cross-referencing this into relevant known sociohistorical or political frameworks that directly influence information output and interpretation. Ethics gets more into interrogating the research and methodological considerations for integrating AI tools. 

Criticality of the “Ethics” domain is directly connected to understanding the role of the researcher as an intermediary in the information ecosystem, as a data gatekeeper in denying or providing access to information, and as a producer of new procedures and practices for navigating the implementation of AI technology. When understood in this light, “Critical Use” is a foundational concept that is encompassed across several keywords.

#### Intertwined Logic of Criticality, Bias, and Ethics

Earlier, I defined “Critical Use” as a bricolage. Borrowed from critical pedagogy, theorist [Joe Kincheloe (2012)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/42981758) developed bricolage to explain the multiple “logics” that can be present in our production of new knowledge and subsequent rationalizations involved in decision-making. When applied to the humanistic component involved in AI implementation, framing “Critical Use” as informational bricolage allows us to forefront and interrogate AI as a standalone tool of engagement, a series of systematic internalized decisions about information order, and a series of interlocked user generated choices which are greatly influenced by a variety of external social factors, cultural demands, and knowledge orientations. Bricolage is directly linked to metaliteracies' challenge “[…] to think about our teaching in new ways and to incorporate metacognitive reflection in learning design” (Jackobsen & Mackey, 2013, pg. 86). Very poignantly, “Critical Use,” just like metaliteracy, seeks to “empower learners to participate in interactive information environments” [(Jackobsen & Mackey, 2013, pg. 88)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1089056).

### Closing Reflection

Drawing upon various knowledge-making frameworks, these projects attempt to build connections between AI literacy and other types of literacies (e.g., informational, algorithmic, functional, ethical, etc.). As highlighted within the description provided by the individual project creators: “These activities promote critical use by encouraging students to reflect on the systemic impact of algorithmic personalization and their own agency in digital environments” (Billington), and “explicitly attempts to demonstrate the uses of AI in research and the potential areas that may be of further concern for researchers. Directly positioning participants to critically engage AI in their research process” (Sara Lowe et al.). All of which effectively “help […] students independently and thoughtfully navigate the opportunities and challenges of generative AI” (Bandel). “Critical Use” attempts to move beyond just acquiring technical proficiency (i.e. digital literacy) and instead to focus on ascertaining knowledge dispositions which interrogates the what, how, and why surrounding AI implementation. These projects offer various frameworks for individuals to resist becoming passive consumers of AI technology.

The seven projects highlighted under “Critical Use” center on the integration of AI into traditional learning and pedagogy. Students and faculty exposed to these various learning objects are invigorated with contextual knowledge of AI that is useful for improving learners’ future decision-making and tool manipulation abilities. Right now, higher education has already seen the rapid expansion of AI, and the market continues to develop. Increasingly, skilled use of AI tools for research and for increasing the efficiency of daily work tasks is just one of the arenas in which AI advancement is projected to intersect with post-graduate marketability and career readiness. AI as a field has also started to open new job opportunities related to business, data analysis, and innovative design within STEM. As we see this expansion, the demand for learners to become educated in how to be critical users of AI is ever-present. I invite instructors to continue exploring AI and creating objects for learning that can be shared and remixed.
