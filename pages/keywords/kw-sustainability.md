### Framing

Sustainability has many definitions, and its meaning has shifted over time. It is often understood as meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs [(World Commission on Environment and Development 1987)](https://sdgs.un.org/sites/default/files/documents/5987our-common-future.pdf). Today, the Sustainable Development Goals, or Global Goals, provide the most widely shared global framework for sustainable development. Unanimously adopted by United Nations Member States in 2015 with a target date of 2030, the 17 goals recognize that environmental sustainability cannot be separated from social and economic issues, including poverty, health, education, gender equality, economic opportunity, peace, justice, and strong institutions. Their guiding commitment to “leave no one behind” places equity at the center of sustainable development [(United Nations 2015)](https://sdgs.un.org/2030agenda).

This holistic understanding of sustainability is particularly relevant as artificial intelligence (AI) technologies evolve rapidly. Learners need to consider more than AI’s direct environmental footprint. They must also examine how its environmental effects intersect with economic and social issues. This means asking who benefits from AI and who bears its costs, who makes decisions about how it is developed and deployed, and which communities have meaningful opportunities to participate in those decisions. Learners must also consider what information is available to evaluate sustainability claims, who is responsible when AI systems contribute to harm, and whether an application that advances one sustainability goal might undermine another.

These questions connect sustainability directly to POEM’s emphasis on critically evaluating information, technologies, and the systems surrounding them. Sustainability literacy enables learners to investigate the evidence behind claims about AI, recognize uncertainty and missing information, identify perspectives that have been excluded, and participate more meaningfully in decisions about responsible technology use. It also encourages learners to examine AI as part of a larger system involving physical infrastructure, natural resources, labor, institutions, public policy, and affected communities.

Current debates about AI data centers illustrate why this broader perspective is necessary. Public discussions often focus on electricity demand, water consumption, and carbon emissions, but data centers are also promoted as sources of employment, investment, tax revenue, and economic development. Their local effects may be especially significant because data-center infrastructure and its resource demands are concentrated in particular communities [(International Energy Agency 2025)](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary). A sustainability perspective considers these effects together. It asks who receives the economic benefits, who experiences environmental burdens or increased utility costs, how durable the promised jobs and investments will be, and who participates in decisions about siting and resource allocation.

Communities also need sufficient information to evaluate these long-term consequences for themselves. Decisions about whether development is sustainable cannot be based solely on projected economic growth or a single environmental measure. These decisions require a consideration of the cumulative effects, tradeoffs, local priorities, community knowledge, and the distribution of benefits and burdens. As AI continues to evolve, these questions will arise in new forms and different places. Educators, learners, and communities therefore need the critical skills to understand these connections, evaluate competing claims, and participate meaningfully in the decisions shaping their futures.

### Context & Scholarship and Critical Considerations

Contemporary understandings of sustainability emerged through more than five decades of international debate about the relationship between environmental protection, human well-being, economic development, and equity. In 1972, representatives of 114 governments gathered for the United Nations Conference on the Human Environment in Stockholm, establishing the environment as an issue of global concern for industrialized and developing countries alike. The concept of “sustainable development” gained prominence through the International Union for Conservation of Nature’s World Conservation Strategy published in 1980, and the 1987 publication of Our Common Future, commonly known as the Brundtland Report. The report defined sustainable development as development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs [(World Commission on Environment and Development 1987)](https://sdgs.un.org/sites/default/files/documents/5987our-common-future.pdf).

The understanding of sustainability broadened considerably at the 1992 United Nations Conference on Environment and Development, or Earth Summit, in Rio de Janeiro. Representatives of 179 countries agreed that sustainable development encompassed social and economic concerns as well as environmental protection. The conference also included a parallel forum for nongovernmental organizations, reflecting growing recognition that governments could not achieve sustainability alone [(United Nations Conference on Environment and Development 1992)](https://www.un.org/en/conferences/environment/rio1992).

In 2000, world leaders adopted the United Nations Millennium Declaration, which established a people-centered approach to development and provided the foundation for the eight Millennium Development Goals. However, subsequent assessments found that the MDG framework was limited by its focus on developing countries, uneven consultation and ownership, and gaps in measurement and accountability. These lessons helped build support for a universal framework developed through a more inclusive process [(United Nations System Task Team 2012)](https://www.un.org/millenniumgoals/pdf/mdg_assessment_Aug.pdf).

At the 2012 United Nations Conference on Sustainable Development, governments launched the process of developing the SDGs. The conference’s outcome document, The Future We Want, called for goals that would integrate the economic, social, and environmental dimensions of sustainability [(United Nations 2012)](https://www.un.org/disabilities/documents/rio20_outcome_document_complete.pdf). The development of the SDGs involved national and thematic consultations, implementation dialogues, and public engagement around the world. Following formal negotiations, United Nations Member States unanimously adopted the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development in September 2015. The agenda established 17 goals and 169 targets intended to guide action through 2030 [(United Nations 2015)](https://sdgs.un.org/2030agenda).

The SDGs marked a significant shift in the global approach to sustainability. They apply to all countries and explicitly connect economic, social, and environmental issues, centering equity. This includes topics such as poverty, health, education, gender equality, environmental protection, economic opportunity, peace, justice, and institutional accountability. Their commitment to “leave no one behind” requires attention not only to overall progress but also to which populations benefit, which experience disproportionate burdens, and which remain excluded from decision-making [(United Nations 2015)](https://sdgs.un.org/2030agenda).

However, sustainability, particularly the notion of  sustainable development, remains a contested concept. The Brundtland definition does not determine which needs should take priority, how competing needs should be balanced, or who has the authority to make those decisions. The SDGs provide a shared framework, but their breadth can make responsibility difficult to assign. National governments adopted the goals, yet progress also depends on local governments, international organizations, universities, businesses, civil society organizations, communities, and individuals. When responsibility is distributed among so many actors, it can become unclear who should be held accountable when progress is slow or policies cause harm.

There is also ongoing tension between the ambition of the goals and whether they can realistically be achieved by 2030. Their universal scope encourages coordinated action, but implementation is voluntary, national circumstances differ, and governments and institutions have unequal resources and capacities. The goals can inspire action even when their targets are not fully achieved, but they can also become symbolic commitments if they are not accompanied by clear responsibilities, transparent reporting, and meaningful community participation. Voluntary Local Reviews and Voluntary University Reviews have emerged as ways for cities and universities to identify their own contributions and gaps, connect local work to global commitments, and expand responsibility beyond national governments [(Carnegie Mellon University n.d.)](https://www.cmu.edu/sustainability-initiative/review/index.html). Businesses also engage through the United Nations Global Compact [(United Nations Global Compact 2005)](https://unglobalcompact.org/about/governance). This policy work provides another avenue for developing critical thinking about sustainability.

The evolution of sustainable development reflects an expanding understanding of both the issues involved and the people entitled to shape them. The SDGs offer a common language for connecting environmental, social, and economic concerns, but their value depends on how they are interpreted and used. Meaningful engagement requires learners to ask whose priorities are represented, whose knowledge counts, what available data reveals or conceals, who participates in decisions, and who is accountable for results. Frameworks that explore topics such as decolonization ecologies, abolition ecology, and environmental justice may also be applied to better understand this topic.

These questions connect sustainability to many of POEM’s other keywords. Data management and stewardship influence which sustainability impacts are measured, how information is preserved and shared, and which communities are visible in the evidence used to make decisions. Evaluation, critical use, and news literacy help learners assess environmental claims, distinguish reliable evidence from unsupported assertions, and identify misleading or incomplete accounts. Disinformation can distort public understanding of environmental effects, while visual literacy helps learners interpret maps, dashboards, charts, photographs, and other representations that may clarify or obscure inequalities.

Sustainability also raises questions about authorship, openness, and ownership. Learners should consider who produced the information or technology they are using, who owns the underlying data and infrastructure, who can inspect or challenge the evidence, and whether affected communities can contribute their own knowledge. Ethics provides a framework for considering responsibilities to present and future generations, while algorithmic bias draws attention to how technological systems may distribute benefits and harms unevenly. Even prompting has sustainability implications because prompts shape how AI systems frame problems, which assumptions they reproduce, which perspectives they include, and what kinds of outputs and resource use they generate.

These connections are especially important when applying sustainability to rapidly developing technologies such as AI, which may contribute to progress on sustainable development challenges while also consuming resources, concentrating decision-making power, reproducing inequalities, and creating new accountability questions. A sustainability perspective therefore asks learners to consider the entire system surrounding a technology, including who develops it, who controls its data, who benefits from its use, who bears its environmental and social costs, and who has the power to change it.

### Closing Reflection

These resources were selected because they demonstrate different ways of understanding the relationship between AI and sustainability while consistently recognizing that sustainability includes environmental, social, and economic factors. They include scholarly publications, interactive exercises, competency frameworks, and classroom materials, creating multiple entry points for students, educators, researchers, and members of the public.

Together, the resources encourage learners to look beyond simple claims about whether AI is environmentally beneficial or harmful. They invite us to examine the larger systems through which AI is developed, powered, governed, and experienced, and to consider how its benefits and burdens are distributed across communities and generations.

Sustainability literacy helps learners connect their own work to broader local and global systems without placing responsibility solely on individual choices. It also prepares them to evaluate evidence, identify missing perspectives, question who has decision-making power, and participate in discussions about technological development.
