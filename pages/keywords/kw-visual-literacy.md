### Framing Visual Literacy

While many of us are used to seeing images as representations of data, not everyone is trained on how to assess the information held within graphs correctly. Lacking the skills to interpret the meaning behind colors and lines or context-informed meaning can lead to the misinterpretation of information. For example, a common trope in American media today is the use of maps to highlight counties as either urban or rural. At first glance, this may seem like a fair way to illustrate where people live in the United States. However, what these maps often fail to show is the population density within each county, which significantly shapes can even distort our understanding of the rural–urban divide. Recognizing that graphs can deliberately or unintentionally obscure important context, such as the role of population density in demographic maps, highlights the importance of visual literacy and the need for individuals to critically evaluate the bias and perspective embedded in both static images and data visualizations.

Visual literacy is the ability to interpret, construct, and critique the effectiveness of data communicated in a visual format, such as images but also graphs, maps, or interactive visualizations. It is an important skill to be able to critically assess and evaluate the context and intent behind images used to exemplify an argument or information within an article or published media. It is also an important skill for those working with data, as the ability to communicate and document the complexities of data analysis quickly for multiple audiences is essential. Without the skills to assess and identify the truth about data from visualizations, it would be difficult for researchers to make much progress in interpreting the work of others. 

POEM is centered on supporting the development of expertise in assessing the underlying biases and complexities of working with information and technology. Being able to address the data within graphs or other visual formats is core to this mission. As data visualizations are increasingly becoming (or in all rights have already become) the shorthand for sharing out data quickly within public-centered media as well as research papers, being able to see behind the lines and colors to ascertain the truth of data is a core skill. This includes being able to identify data sources from graphs, authorial intent within visuals, limitations in data sharing due to chart design, and address the context needed for information within graphs. Visual literacy is foundational to developing these competencies and engaging responsibly with interpreting the hidden values in images and data-driven communication.

### Context & Scholarship and Critical Considerations

The phrase visual literacy generally refers to the ability of people to be able to assess, contextualize, and critique information shown in a visual format. This can include images, but also charts, maps, and interactive visualizations. Often this requires individuals to not just view images but read visualizations, or to deconstruct the elements of an image and then to assess or assign meaning to the components [Thompson et al. 22](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1051144X.2022.2053819?casa_token=iceoYN1BKvgAAAAA:_nSsxcX7pniF8aTxt6U94xlOOx8qTv0C1RMgqeJruFL0PrZ-hdawLz4C_aQLuwJMcJDlzzHwip0k). While image literacy tends to focus on interpreting photographs, artwork, or symbolic imagery for meaning and representation, data visualization literacy specifically emphasizes understanding how quantitative information is encoded visually, requiring attention to scales, axes, data sources, statistical framing, and design choices that shape interpretation.

Within library contexts, work has been done to define visual literacy in terms of image or media literacy (see POEM sections on Media Literacy). The [Association of College & Research Libraries 2022](https://acrl.libguides.com/IRIG/frameworkforvisualliteracy) has released a white paper updating their field setting treaties on information literacies to include visual literacy. It defines visual literacy as “a set of abilities that enables an individual to effectively find,interpret, evaluate, use, and create images and visual media”, allowing a learner to understand, analyse, and contextualize the various ethical, cultural, intellectual, technical, and aesthetic components of visuals [Association of College & Research Libraries 2022; P.2](https://acrl.libguides.com/IRIG/frameworkforvisualliteracy). Much of the critique also centers on the ethical deconstruction of graphs, examining both potential bias in the creator’s intent and how audiences perceive and interpret the visual within its context of use and meaning. 

Although images and data visualizations apply information in different ways, both hold meaningful value in relation to one another. They serve as the link between raw data and an audience’s ability to quickly interpret meaning from visual elements. Both aspects must be considered in tandem to develop a complete understanding of what it means to be literate in this area.

#### Image Literacy

As visual literacy increasingly becomes a necessary skill in both academic research across disciplines and the professional world, it is essential to examine the nuances of how visuals are used in different contexts and to consider how they are incorporated into teaching practices [Kędra 2022](google.com/url?q=https://brill.com/display/book/9789004530928/BP000010.xml&sa=D&source=docs&ust=1771383470460840&usg=AOvVaw3ySQQjbgfNCR76qeXYqN5D). There are some who erroneously conflate media and visual literacy, trusting that individuals familiar with assessing online content will be equally adept at determining the information represented in an image [Pavlounis et al. 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20004508.2023.2292828). Some focus solely on the idea of manipulated images rather than applying the principles of visual literacy as a critical practice for evaluating an image’s context, intent, and content. Discussion of  the context of interpreting images, and understanding the authorial intent and context of image-and not data- use within different centers of media, is still a developing field and viewed as novel activity within college classrooms [Jaekel 2023](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781003444480-10/pedagogical-strategies-developing-visual-literacy-social-justice-kathryn-jaekel); [Kędra 2022](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004530928/BP000010.xml). As part of this development, many are now generating learning content that supports the connection of critical evaluation to images. 

Although students are surrounded by visual media and regularly communicate through images, this constant exposure does not automatically equip them with the skills needed to critically analyze and interpret visual content [Pavlounis et al. 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20004508.2023.2292828). Jaekel details some of the challenges facing today's students, who may have spent so much time consuming visual data through screens that the critical evaluation of images in other contexts might be hard to ascertain, such as an assignment challenging students to find meaning in visual signage on campus and coming up with basic interpretations. For example, the presence of a welcome sign meaning students should feel welcome on campus [Jaekel 2023](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781003444480-10/pedagogical-strategies-developing-visual-literacy-social-justice-kathryn-jaekel). To expand on literacies of dissecting value from images, Jaekel has her students address the position of cultural centers on campus and explore the bias inherent in cultural centers, like how resources for BIPOC communities are shifted to the outer edges. She encourages them to ask who campus is designed for, and how that is shown through the spatial representation of campus. 

Visual representation has also been a central theme in evaluating  images. Emphasizing social justice as a component of image critique, and examining who is included or excluded within a visual space, is essential to developing a more critical and equitable understanding of representation [Thompson et al. 22](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1051144X.2022.2053819?casa_token=iceoYN1BKvgAAAAA:_nSsxcX7pniF8aTxt6U94xlOOx8qTv0C1RMgqeJruFL0PrZ-hdawLz4C_aQLuwJMcJDlzzHwip0k). There is also a growing movement to frame visual literacy as part of civic literacy, emphasizing how the circulation of misleading or inaccurate images can undermine political discourse [Pavlounis et al. 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20004508.2023.2292828). The influx of AI image generation of both popular figures and marginalized communities, either for or against, has increased the need for a critical response to visual representation and the importance of learning objects that focus on visual literacy [Thomson et al. 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1051144X.2025.2566592). Together, these developments underscore that visual image literacy is not merely an interpretive skill, but a civic and ethical imperative in an era of rapidly evolving visual technologies.

#### Data Visualization Literacy 

While many focus on the early stages of the research lifecycle when discussing data as a part of projects, an essential component to any work is the ability of individuals to share their findings with others. The term ‘Data Storytelling’ has become increasingly popular when applied to the creation of data visuals, as individuals have come to understand that being able to build effective visual stories is a component of any information sharing. However, further efforts are needed to clearly define what visual literacy entails and many librarians are still creating and refining materials designed to effectively develop this skill in learners.

Deciding which aspects of data are most important to communicate is an essential component of graph design. Different data types, both quantitative and qualitative, call for different forms of graphs [Cario 2012](https://search.worldcat.org/title/The-functional-art/oclc/824876085). The style or layout of a graph can act as a signpost for interpretation and also indicate to designers how they themselves should construct information in a visual pane. For example, line graphs are typically used to display changes over time and many non-experts approach them with that expectation [Cario 2019](https://wwnorton.com/books/9781324001560). Representing data that has no connection to time as a line graph can then play with an audience's trained expectations for graph use. Similarly, when using color, if you show a graph of the United States where the colors blue and red are used the content will be assumed to be political for local audiences [Furrer et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41235-023-00465-2). Understanding these both formal and informal graph conventions, and the context of audience interpretation, is a vital component of visual literacy. 

The act or art of creating visualizations is also a core component of visual literacy [Thompson et al. 22](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1051144X.2022.2053819?casa_token=iceoYN1BKvgAAAAA:_nSsxcX7pniF8aTxt6U94xlOOx8qTv0C1RMgqeJruFL0PrZ-hdawLz4C_aQLuwJMcJDlzzHwip0k). Through creation learners are able to assess successful strategies for graph design and critique. Like the creation of static images, producing data visualizations can be understood as the ability to design content with a clear purpose and intended audience, using design principles to strengthen communication, convey meaning, and demonstrate proficiency with a variety of digital tools for visual creation [Bombara and Ran 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1051144X.2024.2420303?scroll=top&needAccess=true#abstracthttps://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1051144X.2024.2420303?scroll=top&needAccess=true#abstract). One issue facing the building and interpreting of graphs is separating the form, or style, of a graph from the function, the data contained within. The human eye is drawn to visuals, color, shape and format are all ways that can draw readers to your graph. However, the effectiveness to how they are employed is the difference between keeping the reader engaged with the data or confused as to how to even begin parsing values from an x or y axis. In many cases, the instinct of graph design is to incorporate too much data into one pane, taking the focus away from the core information that should be the main focus of the graph. Methods like the data ink ratio can help instruct on how to keep graphs targeted to capture the data story through limiting ephemera in graphs [Tufte 1997](https://www.edwardtufte.com/book/visual-explanations-images-and-quantities-evidence-and-narrative/?gad_source=1&gad_campaignid=716406692&gbraid=0AAAAADQzVQm7hEcMmiww-HZga275oYtvI&gclid=CjwKCAiAncvMBhBEEiwA9GU_fuWpT27LNJly1yYdJDfXHwrI5AdAkc6QZ455qgPMp1lTKNwXAMf3shoCQZEQAvD_BwE). By helping learners identify the core data they wish to share, it becomes easier for them to determine which graph elements are most essential and to apply the principles of effective visual design and clear data communication.

Authorial intent is also contingent on awareness of one's audience. Identifying the targeted audience for the information is a key aspect of determining what context is needed to make graph building or interpreting effective. Much like with information literacy [ACRL 2016](https://www.ala.org/acrl/standards/ilframework), how the author constructs a narrative  can color an audience's view of data . However, this means that there is always the opportunity for bias or misrepresentation to affect audience interpretation. When critiquing graphs, it is important to consider what the data story is, what the end takeaway should be, and if that is effectively communicated through visual cues or if it is hidden in the graph. Similarly, graph authors should be aware of the cultural contexts of their audience, their expectations for what data needs to be included, and how much context they need to provide to ensure full understanding. Questions to ask while designing graphs include: Are graphs organized in an English first frame - or orienting data from left to right? Are the colors or symbols used relevant to the audience reading them? Are the terms used within the graph accessible to the intended audience? These questions can help to determine what barriers need to be overcome within visually communicating information. 

Work has been done to make graphs more accessible to different communities, including both color blind [Cao 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09332480.2025.2560278?casa_token=NH4u-YL3_WwAAAAA%3AItwNFlXAUekwd8NMmfgNpRAW7YsHhIjvYwby5aD_MOHWNm-0wtzburqrsAtwGXnAWF7vwWQq4ep_), neurodivergent [Elavsky et al. 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/cgf.14522?casa_token=HscOXgSOmT0AAAAA%3A2CetJnwHtcfxlauST7V50obiRiaSBv1rStAkj3b-13Y9y7_f4i8BNGM6QgwHfaHlwhWrE7SQBUI1k4M), and technical and non-technical audiences [Bombara and Ran 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1051144X.2024.2420303?scroll=top&needAccess=true#abstract). Going beyond simple interpretation, different audiences may react differently to how data is expressed based on their own interpretation of the data. For example, one graph I show in my own teaching is focused on describing the mean happiness of various countries. During one instructional session, a student commented that they felt uncomfortable with a graph in which the mean was illustrated by shading smiley faces representing each country, with darker colors assigned to countries that are not majority white. They found this troubling. Although I am certain this was not the author’s intention, it demonstrates how individual perspectives and biases can shape the interpretation of visual data.

### Closing Reflection

I selected these resources because, taken together, they approach data visualization not simply as a technical skill, but as a literacy practice grounded in interpretation, ethics, and communication. Each set of materials supports the development of core competencies in critically evaluating existing visuals while also building the skills necessary to design clear, effective, and ethically responsible data visualizations. Collectively, they foreground essential elements of visual literacy, authorial intent, bias, context, audience, and representation, encouraging learners to see visualizations as constructed arguments rather than neutral displays of information.

Developing fluency in reading and producing visualizations strengthens not only analytical reasoning but also civic literacy. When learners can interrogate how data are framed, whose perspectives are centered, and what actions are encouraged, they are better prepared to participate thoughtfully in democratic and community life. By continuing to ask critical questions surrounding the information we see, we can encourage learners to further develop their ability to interpret data and communicate data to different audiences.
