### Framing

From the minute we wake up in the morning, we all make choices. What we eat for breakfast, what news channels we consume, what transportation we use, who we spend time with, who we support, who we criticize, what we read, what we watch, how we do our work, what we buy, which web browser we use, which AI platforms we use, what teams we support, what political actions we take, what we choose to believe and more. How do we choose? 

To make ethical choices, we cannot rely solely on our feelings, the law or what is acceptable to society: ethics are about what “humans ought to do, usually in terms of rights, obligations, benefits to society, fairness, or specific virtues” [(Markkula Center for Applied Ethics, 2010)](https://www.scu.edu/ethics/ethics-resources/ethical-decision-making/what-is-ethics/). Ethical practice requires a continuous effort of reflecting on our own beliefs, choices, and actions. Reflection is important to ethical choices because our instinctual reactions are often not the best ethical responses. Critical reflection means exploring your own thoughts, feelings, and experiences from different perspectives and identifying how those perspectives might align (or not) with your own perspective [(The Empowered Educator 2018)](https://theempowerededucatoronline.com/2018/06/critical-reflection-for-educators.html/).

Ethics are often thought of as sets of principles (beliefs), standards of behavior, or community agreements that result from deliberations or debate amongst a group or society. They are commonly shared as frameworks or policies that codify these beliefs into ways to be, ways to act. In an essay called “Ethics in a Nutshell”, the [Center for Journalism Ethics (2026)](https://ethics.sjmc.wisc.edu/resources/ethics-in-a-nutshell/) defines ethics as “the dynamic, evolving activity of applying, balancing, and modifying principles in light of new facts, new technology, new social attitudes and changing economic and political conditions.” In other words, when you are confronted with a choice, you *apply* your own belief system (principles) and then *balance* those beliefs with standards/policies from your school or work place, or your community. Sometimes your beliefs or the community standards need to be *modified* because they are outdated, in conflict, or incompatible with current economic, political or other situations. This is where reflection can help.

Ethics as a metaliteracy is concerned chiefly with digital environments and technologies. How can we be good digital citizens? As consumers, how do we trust what we see in the news or in our social environments? How can we spot objects created by AI? As producers, how can we share information without misleading others? Should we disclose when we use AI for our work or projects? These questions are central to ethical practice in our daily lives as information consumers and producers.

### Context & Scholarship and Critical Considerations

Ethics is certainly not a new topic. It has been studied by philosophers for millennia and more recently by scholars of every discipline as they work through how to apply ethics to the very real world problems of their field. Students *need* instruction in ethics [(Barnfield 2018, emphasis added)](https://doi.org/10.22329/celt.v10i0.4752). The complex mix of personal, professional, and social responsibility [(Schultz 2014)](https://www.proquest.com/eric/scholarly-journals/teaching-assessing-ethics-social-responsibility/docview/1651855241/sem-2?accountid=9902) that results from ethical dilemmas can be tricky to navigate without help. According to moral philosophy, ethics is a mode of inquiry that allows us to both pause our intuitive reactions and then consider them alongside the reactions and reasons of others [(Bietti 2020)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3351095.3372860). Teaching this reflective practice is key to ethical training. 

#### Ethics in the curriculum

Ethics are not taught in a uniform way across high school or the university. In a discussion of chemistry curricula, [Shultz (2014)](https://www.proquest.com/eric/scholarly-journals/teaching-assessing-ethics-social-responsibility/docview/1651855241/sem-2?accountid=9902) acknowledges a lack of ethics training in chemistry and other science curricula. In many cases, there is a complete lack of instruction on ethics or ethics is given cursory treatment at the beginning or end of a course. Some instructors endorse building an ethos of ethics embedded across the curriculum [(Barnfield 2018)](https://doi.org/10.22329/celt.v10i0.4752). Others agree but add that ethics cannot be effectively taught as a stand-alone course [(Bush et al. 2009)](https://doi.org/10.19030/cier.v2i4.1068). After many years of teaching stand-alone ethics courses, Delft University of Technology’s Engineering Program is developing “ethics learning lines” across departments [(Doorn 2021)](https://doi.org//10.18260/3-1-1153-25254). These “lines” leverage online materials, e.g., specific case studies that can be embedded across courses in the standard curriculum at the undergraduate level, and include topics like scientific integrity, climate ethics, and ethics of robotics [(Doorn 2021)](https://doi.org//10.18260/3-1-1153-25254). While business schools often have well established ethics programs, entrepreneurship seems to be a grey area. [McCormick et al. (2018)](https://www.proquest.com/eric/scholarly-journals/ethics-entrepreneurship-should-we-be-teaching/docview/2155994405/sem-2?accountid=9902) wrestle with the question of ethics in entrepreneurship. They describe conflicts between ethical practice and the entrepreneurial mindset and push for more emphasis on ethics in the entrepreneurship classroom. 

#### Ways to teach ethics

Ethics can be learned in a variety of ways. The most common way is to use case studies. Good case studies involve complex issues with no clear right or wrong ([Schultz 2014](https://www.proquest.com/eric/scholarly-journals/teaching-assessing-ethics-social-responsibility/docview/1651855241/sem-2?accountid=9902), [Barnfield 2018](https://doi.org/10.22329/celt.v10i0.4752)). Case studies allow learners to see multiple viewpoints and engage with a real problem. Role play, debate and discussion are techniques for using case studies and instructors should aim for a deeper engagement with both ethics and social justice [(Shultz 2014)](https://www.proquest.com/eric/scholarly-journals/teaching-assessing-ethics-social-responsibility/docview/1651855241/sem-2?accountid=9902). Others argue that ethics must be experienced and recommend strategies like service learning to allow students to build empathy and engage in a real community with real dilemmas [(Bush et al. 2009)](https://doi.org/10.19030/cier.v2i4.1068). For the sciences, while most agree that ethics education is important, how to approach actual instruction on ethics or determining what the goals of ethics education should be are still open questions [(Antes et al. 2009)](https://doi.org/10.1080/10508420903035380). No matter how ethics are taught, learners should be trained in and given time for reflection—on cases, before or after debate, and at the end of service learning [(Harvey et al. 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1080/07294360.2025.2463517). 

#### Microethics vs macroethics

Microethics including topics like plagiarism or data fabrication are more commonly taught by instructors [(Schultz 2014)](https://www.proquest.com/eric/scholarly-journals/teaching-assessing-ethics-social-responsibility/docview/1651855241/sem-2?accountid=9902). Most syllabi have a statement about academic conduct tucked at the end but will ‘not cheating’ lead to broader ethical conduct? Unlikely. [Bush et al. (2009)](https://doi.org/10.19030/cier.v2i4.1068), writing from a military college, argue that an honor code that students must uphold can lead to students having integrity which in turn improves their ethical decisions: their study had mixed results. Macroethics, on the other hand, include things like climate change, war, or social justice. Teaching students about macroethics is essential to guide the next generation of professionals to behave ethically and be socially responsible, whereas, a focus on microethics—although they may be more straightforward decisions—often leads to a shallow teaching of ethics [(Schultz 2014)](https://www.proquest.com/eric/scholarly-journals/teaching-assessing-ethics-social-responsibility/docview/1651855241/sem-2?accountid=9902).

#### AI Ethics

In recent years we have seen an AI ethics boom [Corrêa et al. 2023](https://www.cell.com/patterns/abstract/S2666-3899(23)00241-6). There is a sea of AI principles from a wide variety of institutions for users and developers to wade through. A common perception about unethical behavior among AI-related technologies relates to rushed releases and implicit bias that is entwined with the choice of inputs and algorithm design [(Klarin et al. 2022)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sres.2994). The availability and evolution of AI and other technologies continue to grow at a fast pace and policymakers struggle to implement regulations before the next update [(Klarin et al. 2022)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sres.2994). In “The ethics of AI ethics”, [Hagendorff (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11023-020-09517-8) declares that ethical documents have no effect on software developer’s decisions and for companies these documents are mainly used for public relations. Philosophers and lawyers from the AI Ethics Robotics Society collated the largest set of AI guidance documents to date and built an interactive dashboard called [Worldwide AI Ethics](https://nkluge-correa.github.io/worldwide_AI-ethics/). The top six principles, represented in over half of the documents are: Transparency and explainability, Reliability and safety, Justice and fairness, Privacy, Accountability, and Freedom and Autonomy [(Corrêa et al. 2023)](https://www.cell.com/patterns/abstract/S2666-3899(23)00241-6).

Can AI tools develop ethics? AI developers wish that AI tools could ‘learn’ ethics so they can shift blame for ethical blunders to the technology itself. In reality these tools learn from biased data and algorithms and are subject to the same challenges as humans when faced with an ethical dilemma. Software development itself is not a profession so it is likely that AI developers absorb/follow the ethics of the company where they work. Given the startup culture of many AI companies, the character of the entrepreneur becomes the character of that small business [(McCormick et al. 2018)](https://www.proquest.com/eric/scholarly-journals/ethics-entrepreneurship-should-we-be-teaching/docview/2155994405/sem-2?accountid=9902). Why does this matter? As noted above, the entrepreneurial mindset to break things and have a successful company is often in conflict with ethical practice. Self-regulation may be beneficial where regulatory actions are unlikely to be effective or lag behind development, and the company makes good on its word but self-regulation can also be used to justify deregulation and market driven governance [(Bietti 2020)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3351095.3372860). This practice of self-regulation without tangible application by companies has been termed “ethicswashing” [(Schultz et al. 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43681-024-00430-9). 

Ethics is an emerging area of this collection. As with other keywords, there are overlapping objects that align with both this and other keywords, and we welcome new contributions.

### Closing Reflection

Ethics is a broad topic relating to nearly every keyword in the POEM catalog. We always have the chance to make an ethical decision. In our everyday lives we encounter information and technologies that make decisions for us or are the result of decisions by their creators. The Teaching and Learning objects in this collection were selected to help instructors and learners grapple with algorithmic bias in AI, judging authority in media content, and assessing how technology collects data about our activities. Learners will use prompts to uncover underlying biases, evaluate case studies related to social media and digital journalism, and learn to reflect on the results and decisions produced. Learners will discover how values are embedded in the tools they use and how these values are influenced by their creators. We encourage you to embrace the messiness and overlapping metaliteracies within an ethical practice. An appropriate closing comes from the resource [Prompting with a Purpose](#): “AI is a powerful tool, but the criteria and ethics are always yours.”
