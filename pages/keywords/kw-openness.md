### Framing Prompting

“...knowledge is nonrivalrous [...] We can share it without dividing it and consume it without diminishing it” [(Suber 2012)](https://direct.mit.edu/books/book/3754/Open-Access).

Often expressed through open access, open data, and open science initiatives, openness refers to approaches to reduce barriers—economic, legal, technical, etc.—to participation and engagement in learning and knowledge-creation. While openness is often framed as a technical condition, something is either open or not, we encourage you to view it as a relational and participatory practice that fosters access, reuse, dialogue, and co-creation. Openness is not just about public availability, but the permissions and infrastructure that enable meaningful reuse and adaptation ([Budapest Open Access Initiative 2002](https://www.budapestopenaccessinitiative.org/read/), [Open Knowledge Foundation](https://opendatahandbook.org/guide/en/what-is-open-data/), [UNESCO 2021](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/recommendation-open-science)).  

There are many reasons to practice openness and promote. When research outputs, data, and learning materials are restricted by paywalls or proprietary platforms, participation is uneven and often limited to those with funds and/or power. Many times excluding the same communities that are being studied. Furthermore, this lack of access adds a layer of opacity to the process that makes it difficult to discover if equitable and ethical practices are taking place. Open access and open data initiatives emerged in response to these exclusions, seeking to expand who can read, contribute to, and benefit from knowledge production [(Suber 2012)](https://direct.mit.edu/books/book/3754/Open-Access). 

Within the context of POEM we understand openness to be one a fundamental part of metaliteracy. [Mackey and Jacobsen (2011)](https://doi.org/10.5860/crl-76r1) define metaliteracy as “ a unified construct that supports the acquisition, production, and sharing of knowledge in collaborative online communities” (p.62).  None of this can be accomplished without openness. Open practices enable and facilitate sharing, fostering collaborative inquiry and reciprocal relationships between institutions and communities. Furthermore, they allow learners to engage with real audiences and interrogate systems of scholarly communication rather than remaining passive recipients [(UNESCO 2021)](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/recommendation-open-science). 

### Context & Scholarship and Critical Considerations

Our current understanding of openness developed alongside the digital networks that made large‑scale sharing technically feasible. Early open access declarations framed openness as both an ethical and practical response to escalating journal prices and restricted circulation of research. The Budapest Open Access Initiative articulated open access as the free availability of scholarly literature coupled with permissions for reuse and redistribution [(Budapest Open Access Initiative 2002)](https://www.budapestopenaccessinitiative.org/read/). Subsequent statements, such as the *Berlin Declaration on Open Access to Knowledge in the Sciences and Humanities*, reinforced the view that openness in scholarship involves not only access but also the ability to copy, distribute, and create derivative works [(Berlin Declaration 2003)](https://openaccess.mpg.de/Berlin-Declaration). 

Parallel developments in the open data and open source (software) movements emphasized reuse as a defining feature of openness. The Open Definition maintained by the Open Knowledge Foundation specifies that open content and data must permit free use, reuse, and redistribution by anyone, with minimal restrictions ([Open Knowledge Foundation](https://opendefinition.org/od/2.1/en/), [Chignard 2013](https://www.paristechreview.com/2013/03/29/brief-history-open-data/)). International frameworks such as the International Open Data Charter articulate define openness as “open by default,” making direct connections between access to data and transparency, civic participation, and accountability [Open Data Charter (2015)](https://opendatacharter.org/principles/). When it comes to research data, openness is often discussed alongside the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which aim to make data easier to use by both humans and machines through metadata, standards, and stewardship [(Wilkinson et al. 2016)](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata201618). While FAIR does require data to be open, it has helped move the open data conversation and practices from just free access to meaningful, well-documented, and reusable access. Regarding software, the *Open Source Definition* frames openness around redistribution, access to source code, and the right to modify and/or share improvements [(Open Source Initiative 2024)](https://opensource.org/osd). Together, these movements built an understanding of openness that prioritizes participation, adaptability, and collective improvement over mere consumption. 

Open science integrates open access, open data, open source software, and open infrastructure into a broader vision of collaborative knowledge production. UNESCO’s *Recommendation on Open Science* defines open science as an approach that aims to make scientific knowledge openly available, accessible, and reusable, while also fostering collaboration, societal engagement, and respect for diverse knowledge systems [(UNESCO 2021)](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000379949.locale=en). Similarly, *Open Science by Design*, published by the U.S. National Academies, highlights openness as essential to transparency, reproducibility, and public trust in research [(National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine 2018)](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/25116/open-science-by-design). These aims are also present in the European Union’s open science policy which positions openness as a means to accelerate discovery, improve research quality, and enhance the societal impact of science by lowering barriers to collaboration and large‑scale knowledge sharing [(European Union)](https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/strategy/strategy-research-and-innovation/our-digital-future/open-science_en).

While openness has been widely endorsed by researchers, institutions, and governments, its implementation has brought about concerts regarding ethics and equity. For example, the ability to create open content relies on funding and/or infrastructure that is often concentrated or more accessible to researchers in the Global North, which could result in open systems reproducing existing inequalities [(Raju et al. 2020)](https://direct.mit.edu/books/oa-edited-volume/4933/Reassembling-Scholarly-CommunicationsHistories). Feminist scholars caution that openness can become extractive when it prioritizes scale and reuse without attention to power, care, and context, particularly where marginalized communities are involved [(D’Ignazio & Klein 2020)](https://data-feminism.mitpress.mit.edu/).  Building on these critiques, feminist scholar Denisse Albornoz calls for radical openness, an approach that rejects openness as a universal or inherently emancipatory condition and instead understands it as politically situated and contingent. From this perspective, openness can both redistribute and reinforce power depending on how it is enacted, foregrounding the importance of risk, consent, refusal, and responsibility. Radical openness emphasizes that open practices should be designed to support meaningful participation and community well‑being rather than default transparency or unrestricted reuse ([Albornoz 2018](https://medium.com/@denalbz/reimagining-open-science-through-a-feminist-lens-546f3d10fa65), [Albornoz et al. 2018](https://elpub.episciences.org/articles/4612)).

These critiques are echoed in Indigenous and anti‑colonial scholarship, which challenges universalist narratives of openness and reflects on whose interests such openness ultimately serves. Indigenous data governance initiatives such as the CARE Principles emphasize Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, and Ethics, offering an alternative framework that centers community sovereignty alongside—and in tension with—open access norms [(Global Indigenous Data Alliance 2019)](https://www.gida-global.org/care). UNESCO’s open science framework explicitly acknowledges these tensions, calling for “diverse paths to open science” that respect and encourage cultural diversity, linguistic plurality, and different epistemologies rather than assuming a single global model [(UNESCO 2021)](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000379949.locale=en).

As you engage with the teaching and learning objects in this section, we encourage you to view openness not as a fixed state but as an ongoing, negotiated practice that is shaped by social values, institutional incentives, and community norms, raising persistent questions about who benefits, who bears risk, and whose knowledge is recognized and sustained.

### Closing Reflection

These teaching and learning objects were selected to reflect openness as both a set of practices and a site of inquiry. Together, they frame openness not only as a technical or policy condition, but as a situated and negotiated practice shaped by disciplinary norms, institutional contexts, and social values. Spanning multiple modalities that include presentations, lesson plans, games, skill‑building activities, and video, these resources highlight the varied ways learners encounter, question, and enact openness across the research and learning lifecycle.

Within POEM, openness supports critical literacies by encouraging learners to move beyond consumption toward participation, reflection, and agency. Engaging with open practices involves evaluating permissions and infrastructure, considering ethical implications, and examining how knowledge circulates and accrues value. These dimensions align closely with metaliteracy’s emphasis on producing, sharing, and interrogating knowledge within collaborative and networked environments rather than accepting scholarly systems as fixed or neutral.

As educators and learners engage with openness, several questions remain central: What is being opened, and for whom? Who benefits from openness in a particular context, and who may bear new risks? When might selective sharing, care, or refusal be necessary companions to openness? How can open practices support participation, accountability, and reuse without reinforcing existing inequities?  We invite readers to treat these resources as starting points—to adapt them, remix them, and place them in dialogue with local needs and community priorities. Openness, as framed here, remains unfinished and continually shaped through practice.
