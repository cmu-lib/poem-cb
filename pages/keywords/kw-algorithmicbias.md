### Framing

In February 2023, the city of Amsterdam rolled out what officials believed was a breakthrough in fair welfare administration. Their "Smart Check" algorithm would screen benefit applications, identifying potentially fraudulent cases while reducing the bias that human caseworkers had historically shown against Dutch nationals and women. The system incorporated every principle from the "responsible AI" playbook: external audits, bias testing, diverse stakeholder consultation, and transparent documentation. Yet despite investing €500,000 and years of development, the pilot revealed that the algorithm merely flipped the bias, now wrongly flagging non-Dutch applicants, men, and parents at higher rates. By November 2023, city officials terminated the project, returning to a human-led process they knew was also biased [(Guo et al., 2025)](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/06/11/1118233/amsterdam-fair-welfare-ai-discriminatory-algorithms-failure/). 

Amsterdam's story captures a fundamental tension in our algorithmic age: can automated decision-making systems ever be truly fair, or do they inevitably encode and amplify the inequities embedded in our social structures? 

[Kordzadeh & Ghasemaghaei (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1080/0960085X.2021.1927212) state that " algorithmic bias “occurs when the outputs of an algorithm benefit or disadvantage certain individuals or groups more than others without a justified reason for such unequal impacts (p. 388).  These biases emerge when algorithms trained on historical data inherit and perpetuate the discriminatory patterns hidden within that data, or when the design choices of developers inadvertently encode their own blind spots and assumptions [(Johnson, 2021)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-020-02696-y).  From facial recognition systems that fail to identify darker-skinned faces to hiring algorithms that penalize women's resumes to welfare systems that disproportionately flag marginalized communities for investigation, algorithmic bias has become a pervasive force shaping access to opportunity, resources, and justice [(Mitchell et al., 2021)](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-statistics-042720-125902). 

For educators and learners navigating metaliteracies in the 21st century, understanding algorithmic bias is essential. We live in a world where algorithms make consequential decisions about college admissions, loan approvals, criminal sentencing, healthcare allocation, employment screening, and social services eligibility [(Eubanks, 2019)](https://virginia-eubanks.com/automating-inequality/). These systems often operate invisibly, cloaked in the language of objectivity and efficiency. Yet they carry within them the power to systematically advantage or disadvantage entire communities, often without meaningful transparency, accountability, or mechanisms for redress [(Manríquez Roa & Biller‐Andorno, 2023)](https://doi.org/10.1111/bioe.13215). Developing critical literacies around algorithmic bias enables us to interrogate these systems, demand accountability from their creators and deployers, and imagine alternative futures in which technology serves justice rather than undermines it.

### Context & Scholarship and Critical Considerations

The scholarly conversation around algorithmic bias has evolved dramatically over the past decade, moving from technical discussions of "fairness metrics" to deeper interrogations of how technology reproduces structural inequities [(Eubanks, 2019)](https://virginia-eubanks.com/automating-inequality/). This evolution has been driven by a convergence of computer science, critical race studies, feminist theory, science and technology studies, and information science. 

#### The Technical Landscape 

Within computer science and machine learning, researchers have identified multiple forms of bias that can enter algorithmic systems [(Jonker & Rodgers, n.d.)](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/algorithmic-bias). Training data bias occurs when the datasets used to teach algorithms reflect historical patterns of discrimination or underrepresent certain groups [(Kordzade & Ghasemaghaei, 2022)](https://doi.org/10.1080/0960085X.2021.1927212). Label bias emerges when proxy measures inadvertently introduce discrimination, as when healthcare algorithms predict costs rather than actual health needs, leading to underprovision of care for Black patients [(Favier et al., 2023)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10994-023-06401-1). Selection bias arises when the data used to train systems is non-representative of the populations they will serve [(Favier et al., 2023)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10994-023-06401-1). 

The technical community has proposed dozens of mathematical definitions of "fairness," yet these definitions are often incompatible with each other [(Wang et al., 2022)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44176-022-00006-z). A system designed to achieve "equality of outcomes" (producing the same result for all groups) may violate "equality of treatment" (considering differences between individuals). This mathematical impossibility means that claims of "unbiased" or "fair" algorithms always involve choices about which form of fairness to prioritize and which groups to protect. 

#### Critical Perspectives: Race, Power, and Justice

The most transformative scholarship on algorithmic bias has come from scholars who situate these technical issues within broader histories of racism, sexism, and structural inequality. Safiya Umoja Noble's groundbreaking 2018 book *[Algorithms of Oppression: How Search Engines Reinforce Racism](https://files.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/6105/files/2019/01/SAFIYA-NOBLE.pdf)* demonstrated how Google search results perpetuated harmful stereotypes about Black women and other marginalized groups. Noble coined the term "algorithmic oppression" (p. 4) to describe data failures specific to people of color and women, arguing that commercial search engines' profit-driven logic systematically disadvantages already marginalized communities. Her work dismantled the myth that search engines are neutral information retrieval systems, revealing instead how they are shaped by corporate interests and encode racist and sexist hierarchies. 

Ruha Benjamin, associate professor of African American Studies at Princeton University and 2021 MacArthur Fellow, developed the concept of the "New Jim Code" in her 2019 book *[Race After Technology: Abolitionist Tools for the New Jim Code](https://www.ruhabenjamin.com/race-after-technology)*. This framework describes discriminatory designs that encode inequity through three mechanisms: systems that explicitly amplify racial hierarchies, those that ignore and thereby replicate social divisions, and those that aim to fix racial bias but ultimately do the opposite. Benjamin's work draws crucial connections between contemporary algorithmic systems and historical technologies of racial control and surveillance, challenging the notion that technology represents neutral or inevitable progress. 

Joy Buolamwini has documented how facial recognition systems fail to recognize darker-skinned faces, particularly Black women. Buolamwini's research revealed that commercial facial recognition systems had error rates below 1% for white men but exceeded 34% for darker-skinned women, leading to wrongful arrests and systematic exclusion from services relying on these technologies [(Lee & Barton, 2019)](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/algorithmic-bias-detection-and-mitigation-best-practices-and-policies-to-reduce-consumer-harms/). 

Cathy O'Neil's *[Weapons of Math Destruction](https://books.google.com/books?hl=en&lr=&id=cbwvDwAAQBAJ&oi=fnd&pg=PA1&dq=Weapons+of+math+destruction%E2%80%AF:+how+big+data+increases+inequality+and+threatens+democracy+&ots=XlBfSXIyXi&sig=Wt4C888cdZO0AfQDX5iIfEtGyB8#v=onepage&q=Weapons%20of%20math%20destruction%E2%80%AF%3A%20how%20big%20data%20increases%20inequality%20and%20threatens%20democracy&f=false)* (2016) demonstrated how opaque algorithmic systems in domains like education, employment, and criminal justice create feedback loops that punish the poor and reinforce inequality. Virginia Eubanks's *[Automating Inequality](https://virginia-eubanks.com/automating-inequality/)* (2019) showed how automated systems in social services operate as "digital poorhouses," (p. 199) intensifying surveillance and control of low-income communities while claiming to increase efficiency. 

#### Key Tensions and Debates 

Several critical debates animate this field. One concerns technological solutionism: can technical fixes like debiasing algorithms or diverse training data solve problems rooted in structural inequity, or do such approaches merely create the illusion of fairness while leaving power structures intact [(Saetra, 2023)](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/62281/1/9781000886078.pdf)? Amsterdam's failed welfare algorithm exemplifies this tension; technical interventions repeatedly failed to address the fundamental question of whether algorithmic profiling of welfare recipients was justifiable at all. 

Another debate centers on transparency versus opacity [(Manríquez Roa & Biller‐Andorno, 2023)](https://doi.org/10.1111/bioe.13215). The proprietary nature of many algorithms creates barriers to accountability, the "black box" problem means that even when systems produce discriminatory outcomes, understanding why is often impossible. Yet some scholars argue that transparency alone is insufficient, as complex algorithms may be technically transparent but still incomprehensible to those affected by them [(Burrell, 2016)](https://doi.org/10.1177/2053951715622512). 

A third tension involves individual versus structural approaches. Should interventions focus on increasing diversity among algorithm developers, creating better auditing mechanisms, or fundamentally questioning whether certain uses of algorithmic decision-making are appropriate at all? Noble, Benjamin, and others in the race critical code studies tradition argue that focusing solely on technical fixes or diversity initiatives without addressing underlying power structures misses the point ([Benjamin, 2019](https://www.ruhabenjamin.com/race-after-technology); [Noble, 2018](https://files.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/6105/files/2019/01/SAFIYA-NOBLE.pdf)). 

#### Marginalized Voices and Colonial Legacies

Feminist and decolonial scholarship has been essential in revealing how algorithmic systems encode not just racial and gender bias, but also colonial logics and Western-centric worldviews [(Mohamed et al., 2020)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-020-00405-8). Research on facial recognition's failures with darker-skinned faces connects to longer histories of how technology has been designed for and by white, Western populations. The dominance of English-language training data and American cultural assumptions in large language models reflects linguistic imperialism and cultural hegemony [(Milan & Trere, 2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1527476419837739). 

Scholars like Simone Browne have traced connections between contemporary surveillance technologies and historical systems of racial control, from slave passes to apartheid passbooks [(Browne, 2015)](https://books.google.com/books?hl=en&lr=&id=snmJCgAAQBAJ&oi=fnd&pg=PA24&dq=simone+browne+dark+matters&ots=taL0E13A0a&sig=I5ksEu7Z64UobcU6-FPLzGYR3cc#v=onepage&q=simone%20browne%20dark%20matters&f=false). This work reminds us that algorithmic bias is not a technical glitch to be fixed, but part of longer trajectories of using technology to monitor, control, and discriminate against marginalized populations. 

#### Adjacent Concepts and Connections 

Algorithmic bias intersects with multiple related concepts that POEM readers might explore. [Aurora Cuevas-Cervero et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.acalib.2023.102786) situate awareness of algorithmic bias as a component of critical information literacy. Digital citizenship demands understanding how our participation in digital spaces is shaped by algorithmic systems. Information literacy requires recognizing that search engines and recommendation algorithms curate what information we encounter. Data justice concerns who collects data, for what purposes, and with what protections [(Zavrsnik, A., 2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1477370819876762 ). Surveillance capitalism describes business models built on extracting and monetizing personal data, often in ways that amplify bias [(Cinnamon, 2017)](https://doi.org/10.24908/ss.v15i5.6433).  

The conversation around algorithmic bias also connects to ongoing struggles over facial recognition bans, predictive policing, automated decision-making in social services, and the regulation of AI systems [(Conger et al., 2019)](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/05/14/us/facial-recognition-ban-san-francisco.html). The [European Union's AI Act (2024)](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu) and calls for algorithmic impact assessments represent efforts to create governance frameworks, yet debates persist about whether regulation can ever adequately address power imbalances encoded in technology. 

### Closing Reflection

Understanding algorithmic bias is not simply about learning to spot technical flaws; it's about developing the capacity to recognize and challenge how power operates through sociotechnical systems. 

The Amsterdam welfare algorithm story that opens this entry reveals a fundamental truth: good intentions, technical sophistication, and even rigorous bias testing do not automatically produce just outcomes. The city's officials genuinely believed they were building a fairer system. They followed best practices, consulted experts, and invested substantial resources. Yet they confronted a reality that scholars like Noble and Benjamin have been articulating for years: algorithmic systems exist within and are shaped by broader structures of inequality that no amount of technical tweaking can fully resolve. 

This doesn't mean we should abandon efforts to make algorithms less harmful. But it does mean we must ask deeper questions: Which problems should we attempt to solve with algorithms at all? Who gets to decide? What forms of human judgment and accountability are we willing to sacrifice in the name of efficiency? How do we center the voices and experiences of communities most harmed by algorithmic systems in decisions about their design and deployment? 

For current learners, algorithmic bias represents both a critical literacy need and an opportunity for engagement. These systems increasingly mediate access to education, employment, healthcare, housing, and justice. Developing the capacity to interrogate them, whether through technical analysis, policy advocacy, community organizing, or creative intervention, is essential for participating meaningfully in contemporary society. 

The keyword "algorithmic bias" supports critical literacies by demanding that we see technology not as neutral or inevitable but as shaped by human choices embedded in particular relations of power. It asks us to look beneath claims of objectivity to examine whose interests are served and whose are harmed. It connects seemingly disparate domains, from search engines to criminal justice to social services, revealing common patterns of how automated decision-making can encode and amplify injustice.
